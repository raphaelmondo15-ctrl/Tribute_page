Dr. Norman Borlaug

The man who saved a billion lives

Dr. Norman Borlaug, third from the left, trains biologists in Mexico on how to increase wheat yield — part of his lifelong war on hunger.

Here's a timeline of Dr. Borlaug's life:

  • 1914 — Born in Cresco, Iowa
  • 1933 — Leaves his family's farm to attend the University of Minnesota, thanks to a Depression-era program known as the National Youth Administration
  • 1935 — Stops school to save money. Works in the Civilian Conservation Corps, helping starving Americans. “I saw how food changed them,” he said. “All of this left scars on me.”
  • 1937 — Finishes university and takes a job in the U.S. Forestry Service
  • 1938 — Marries Margaret Gibson. Gets laid off due to budget cuts. Inspired by Elvin Charles Stakman, he returns to school to study under Stakman, who teaches him about breeding pest-resistant plants.
  • 1941 — Tries to enlist in the military after the Pearl Harbor attack but is rejected. Instead, the military asks his lab to work on waterproof glue, DDT for malaria control, disinfectants, and other applied science.
  • 1942 — Receives a Ph.D. in Genetics and Plant Pathology
  • 1944 — Rejects a 100% salary increase from DuPont, leaves behind his pregnant wife, and flies to Mexico to lead a new plant pathology program. Over 16 years, he and his team breed over 6,000 strains of disease-resistant wheat suited for major climates worldwide.
  • 1945 — Discovers a way to grow wheat twice per year, doubling yields
  • 1953 — Crosses a short, sturdy dwarf wheat with a high-yield American breed, creating a strain that responds well to fertilizer. It eventually provides 95% of Mexico’s wheat.
  • 1962 — Brings his high-yielding wheat strains to the Indian subcontinent, helping prevent mass starvation
  • 1970 — Receives the Nobel Peace Prize
  • 1983 — Helps seven African countries dramatically increase their maize and sorghum yields
  • 1984 — Becomes a distinguished professor at Texas A&M University
  • 2005 — States that “we will have to double the world food supply by 2050.” Argues for the importance of genetically modified crops, noting that humans have been modifying plants through selective breeding for centuries.
  • 2009 — Dies at age 95

“Borlaug's life and achievement are testimony to the far-reaching contribution that one man's towering intellect, persistence, and scientific vision can make to human peace and progress.”

— Indian Prime Minister Manmohan Singh
